### 2. Detailed Discussion of Key Periods

#### 2.1 The Dollar Peg Era (2000-2008)

Throughout 2000-2008, the National Bank of Ukraine maintained what Calvo and Reinhart (2002) would classify as a textbook case of "fear of floating." While the de jure regime was described as "managed floating," the National Bank of Ukraine intervened heavily in the foreign exchange market to maintain the hryvnia within a narrow band around the United States dollar. The rate stabilised at approximately Ukrainian hryvnia 5.4/United States dollar after the 1998-99 crisis and was formalised as an official peg at Ukrainian hryvnia 5.05/United States dollar on 21 April 2005. During this period, the impossible trinity was binding: with the exchange rate anchored and capital flows increasingly open, the National Bank of Ukraine effectively surrendered independent monetary policy. Interest rates were subordinated to the exchange-rate target, and domestic inflation, which averaged 9-12% annually and spiked above 25% in 2008, was largely determined by external commodity prices and the expansionary fiscal stance, not by active National Bank of Ukraine policy.

#### 2.2 The 2008-2009 Crisis and Forced Devaluation

The global financial crisis triggered a sudden stop in capital flows and a collapse in steel and commodity export revenues. The National Bank of Ukraine was forced to abandon the peg in October 2008, and the hryvnia depreciated approximately 38% in under three months (from Ukrainian hryvnia 4.85 to Ukrainian hryvnia 7.88/United States dollar). A new de facto peg was established at approximately Ukrainian hryvnia 8.0/United States dollar, which would hold until 2014. This episode demonstrated the fundamental vulnerability of Ukraine’s peg regime: the exchange rate served as a shock absorber of last resort, but only through disorderly, large devaluations rather than gradual adjustment. Foreign exchange reserves, which had peaked near $38 billion, were depleted rapidly. An International Monetary Fund Stand-By Arrangement was activated to support the balance of payments.

#### 2.3 The 2014-2015 Crisis: Euromaidan, Crimea, and the Float

The most dramatic regime change in the sample period occurred in February 2014. Following the Euromaidan revolution and the Russian annexation of Crimea, capital flight accelerated and the National Bank of Ukraine was forced to allow the hryvnia to float (7 February 2014). The currency depreciated from Ukrainian hryvnia 8.15/United States dollar to a record low of approximately Ukrainian hryvnia 33/United States dollar by late February 2015, a cumulative depreciation exceeding 70%. This crisis marked the definitive end of the dollar-peg era. The National Bank of Ukraine introduced severe capital controls in March 2015, including a 75% mandatory foreign exchange sale requirement and a ban on dividend repatriation. Year-on-year inflation reached 60.9% in April 2015, the highest in the sample. A $17.5 billion International Monetary Fund Extended Fund Facility was approved in March 2015, conditional on the National Bank of Ukraine’s commitment to adopt inflation targeting.

#### 2.4 Inflation Targeting Adoption (2015-2022)

The National Bank of Ukraine formally adopted an inflation-targeting framework on 18 August 2015 (Board Resolution No. 541).[^4] The initial target was 8% plus or minus 2 percentage points, with a convergence path toward 5% by 2019-2020. The National Bank of Ukraine’s institutional independence was strengthened by law in September 2015. This represents the only sustained period in the sample where Ukraine exercised genuine monetary sovereignty: the key policy rate became the primary instrument, the exchange rate floated (though with occasional smoothing interventions), and capital controls were progressively eased. By 2019-2020, inflation had converged near the 5% target, a remarkable achievement. The monetary transmission mechanism functioned through the interest rate channel, as documented by Nikolaychuk and Shpanel (2016) and subsequent National Bank of Ukraine research on neutral rates (Grui, Lepushynskyi, and Nikolaychuk, 2018).

#### 2.5 Wartime Fixed Rate and Return to Managed Flexibility (2022-present)

Russia’s full-scale invasion on 24 February 2022 prompted the National Bank of Ukraine to suspend inflation targeting and fix the exchange rate at Ukrainian hryvnia 29.25/United States dollar as an emergency anchor.[^5] Comprehensive capital controls were reimposed (Resolution No. 18). On 21 July 2022, the rate was devalued by 25% to Ukrainian hryvnia 36.57/United States dollar to reduce the gap with the parallel market. Inflation peaked near 27% in late 2022. From 3 October 2023, the National Bank of Ukraine transitioned to managed flexibility, allowing the rate to be determined in the interbank market. A revised "flexible inflation targeting" framework was adopted in September 2024 with a 5% target and an extended 3-year policy horizon.[^6] Capital controls continue to be eased gradually.

### 3. Monetary Sovereignty and Implications for the Counterfactual

The chronology reveals a critical asymmetry for the counterfactual exercise. Ukraine had **actual** monetary sovereignty for only a brief window (approximately 2016-2022) when the inflation-targeting framework was operational and the exchange rate floated. During the long dollar-peg periods, the National Bank of Ukraine’s monetary policy was *de facto constrained by the exchange-rate anchor*. Under the peg, the impossible trinity bound: with open (or opening) capital flows, fixing the exchange rate meant forfeiting independent interest-rate policy. In these periods, Ukraine’s monetary regime was already functionally similar to a fixed exchange-rate arrangement.

This has a direct implication for the counterfactual treatment of Euro Area membership. During the peg periods, hypothetical euro adoption would not have represented a large change in monetary regime, as Ukraine was already constrained by the peg. The treatment is therefore concentrated in the episodes where Ukraine deviated from its peg: the three major devaluation crises (2008, 2014-2015, 2022) and the post-2016 inflation-targeting period. Under Euro Area membership, these devaluations would have been impossible; adjustment would instead have occurred through internal devaluation (wage and price deflation), as experienced by Greece, Ireland, and the Baltic states.
